§02 · Recall block

Seven things to carry into the room without notes

  1. The circulated memo's arithmetic is correct and its conclusion is still wrong. Every multiple in it ties out to the cent: 2.44x at $100B, 6.10x at $250B, 12.20x at $500B. It reaches the wrong answer because three inputs that decide the outcome — the instrument, the fee stack and the holding period — were never entered.
  2. We are not being offered the cap table. JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, BlackRock, DST Global and ARCH bought the Series B directly at a $29B pre-money with information rights. What is available to us is indirect exposure through an unaffiliated vehicle whose sponsor states it is not acting as broker-dealer, placement agent, adviser or fiduciary.
  3. An unapproved transfer can be worth zero regardless of the company's value. OpenAI's published policy voids non-compliant transfers outright and says they "will not be recognized and carry no economic value to you," naming SPVs specifically. Anthropic has taken the same posture. Prometheus is fifteen months old with no employee liquidity event and no float; secondary platforms state company approval and a right of first refusal apply.
  4. The company discloses no revenue, no shipped product, no named paying customer, no benchmark, no technical publication and no product timeline. The only customer relationship named on the record is Blue Origin, described by Bezos as "a case study for a customer" — a related party he owns.
  5. The data the entire thesis depends on does not exist yet, and the company says so: there is no internet of manufacturing data. The plan to obtain it is a separate reported $100B vehicle to acquire industrial companies, which has no named backers and has not closed.
  6. The exit multiple is capped by a category the market is marking down. The four listed engineering-software companies of scale trade at 4.0x to 8.6x revenue and are worth about $170B combined. Prometheus at $41B is roughly a quarter of that entire enterprise value, on zero revenue.
  7. The relevant base rate is Blue Origin, not Amazon. Bezos's one prior capital-intensive physical-engineering company took a reported $28B of his own money over 26 years, started two years before its focused competitor, and has three lifetime orbital launches against roughly 660, with its only operational pad out of action since May 2026 and no liquidity ever returned to an outside investor — because until July 2026 it had none.

§04 · What the company actually does

An AI research laboratory, fifteen months old, building a design tool

About 150 people across San Francisco, London and Zurich, plus a 100,000 square foot manufacturing lease in West Oakland signed in August 2026 EXT. The stated objective is an "artificial general engineer": a system that learns from real-world physical experiment rather than only from digital corpora, and that can design, simulate, test and ultimately help manufacture complex physical products — jet engines, semiconductors, medical devices, vehicles.

It is worth stating plainly what the principals themselves have said the product is, because the circulated memo frames it more expansively than they do. In May 2026 Bezos described it as "a very, very modern version" of CAD and said the company has "nothing to do with robotics" EXT. The headline ambition is the productivity claim he gave on CNBC on 11 June 2026: something that "was going to take 100 engineers 10 years to build," done instead by "10 engineers one year" CO.

The company has made one acquisition, General Agents, in November 2025 — a video-language-action agent team whose founders include a co-author of the original GAN paper and a former OpenAI research scientist EXT. Beyond that there is no public artefact: no paper, no model card, no benchmark, no demonstration. As one trade outlet put the position, this is a company that raised ten figures without a prototype.

§05 · Analogy anchor

Start with the toy version

The company's implicit analogy is AlphaFold for hardware. Take a domain where human expertise is the bottleneck, learn the underlying structure from data, and collapse a multi-year discovery cycle into a computation. That analogy is the strongest argument in the file and it is why serious institutions wrote the cheque.

Where it breaks is the evaluation function. Protein folding has a cheap, fast, objective scoring signal and a digital manufacturing step. Jet engines have neither. The evaluation is a multi-month physical test campaign followed by a multi-year certification process, and the thing being evaluated cannot be simulated to the required fidelity — which is precisely why the programmes take a decade.

The better analogy is Blue Origin, and it is not flattering. Same founder, same pattern: correctly identified opportunity, effectively unlimited capital, a genuinely hard physical domain, and a competitor who gets the iteration data as a by-product of a business they already operate. See §10 and §12.

§06 · Market, rebuilt from the bottom

Rebuild it from enterprises and contract value, not from category reports

The circulated memo anchors on a $58.4B engineering-software market growing to $120.7B by 2034, a $41.9B industrial-robotics market, and a constructed $60–100B software TAM EXT. Those published figures check out. The problem is that a category total tells you nothing about who captures it, at what share, at what margin, or when. Co-founder Bajaj has separately cited a market of roughly $70 trillion, about 60% of world GDP CO. That is not a market size; it is the denominator of the economy.

Rebuild it the way the vault rebuilds any market: count the enterprises that could plausibly buy, assign a contract value, and pick a penetration.

§10 · Competitive position and the capital gap

At $18.2B raised, Prometheus is better capitalised than any competitor in its specific niche. That is not the binding constraint. The binding constraint is that the scarce input is proprietary physical data, and every serious competitor obtains it as a by-product of a business they already operate, while Prometheus must buy it.

§11 · Moat under model-capability doubling

Has an AI ever designed a complex physical thing that was built and worked?

Yes — and the pattern in where it worked is the most important technical finding in this memo. Autonomous AI design has produced artefacts that were built and functioned only where three conditions hold simultaneously: the design space is fully digital, the evaluation function is cheap, fast and objective, and manufacturing is itself digital. Chips, code and, to a degree, proteins satisfy all three.

Outside those conditions, the two landmark claims have both been substantially rebutted. A UCSD re-implementation of Google's AlphaChip found it did not outperform established techniques; Nature retracted the accompanying commentary in September 2023; and we could find no published independent replication EXT. On GNoME, independent reviewers reported they could identify no strikingly novel compounds among the proposed structures, most being minor variants of known materials EXT. In drug discovery, the flagship AI-designed candidates missed their efficacy endpoints or were discontinued EXT. In mechanical CAD there is no credible evidence at all: the best-funded incumbent attempt produces crude, non-parametric geometry.

Prometheus's own headline example sits at the far end of the hard case. A jet engine programme is long because of qualification and physical test, not because engineers cannot draw. Compressing the drawing tenfold does not compress the programme tenfold. Aerospace and medical-device certification regimes will not accept AI-generated designs quickly; adoption in those markets is measured in years.

Under model-capability doubling the moat question inverts in an uncomfortable way. If frontier models get much better and cheaper, the design-generation layer Prometheus is building becomes commoditised and the durable advantage moves entirely to whoever holds the proprietary test-and-qualification corpus. That is Siemens, Synopsys and the manufacturers themselves. The better frontier models get, the more the value accrues to the data holder rather than the model builder — which is precisely why the $100B acquisition vehicle exists, and precisely why its failure to close is the most important open item in the file.

§13 · Go-to-market credibility

The hardest enterprise sale there is, with nothing yet to sell

The target buyers are aerospace, defence, semiconductor, automotive, energy and pharmaceutical engineering organisations. These are the slowest-validating customers in the economy, and for good reasons: mistakes are expensive and sometimes fatal. Certification regimes in aerospace and medical devices will not accept AI-generated designs on a software procurement timetable. Adoption in these markets is measured in years and gated on qualification evidence that does not yet exist.

Three structural obstacles, none of which capital solves:

  • Trust and traceability. An engineering organisation must be able to defend a design to a regulator and to its own liability insurers. "The model proposed it" is not a defence. Validation dominates generation, and validation is where the cost sits.
  • Change propagation. A design change cascades through drawings, bills of material, supplier quotations, tooling and inspection plans. Systems that demo well tend to fail here, and this is the part no public artefact addresses.
  • Incumbent bundling. Siemens, Dassault, PTC, Autodesk and Synopsys can attach AI to contracts these customers already hold, at zero switching cost, and they are all shipping. Prometheus must displace a system of record.

Against this, the company has no named paying customer, no disclosed pilot, no reference design win and no product date. The one relationship on record is with a company the founder owns. Scored 3.0, and the score would not move on a customer logo alone — it would move on a customer result.

Bear42%Two paths, both ending near zero for us. Either the evaluation bottleneck proves binding, capital is consumed, and the outcome is the modal one for a pre-revenue laboratory with excellent people — an acqui-hire or licence deal that makes direct investors nominally whole (Adept returned about 1.0x, Inflection 1.1–1.5x) and, after a three-layer fee stack, returns a fraction of that to us. Or the transfer is never recorded on the company's cap table and the interest is void, which is a total loss independent of how Prometheus performs.

Three things to take from the sliders. First, drag layers from 2 to 0 and hold everything else: the same company outcome pays materially more direct than through the marketed vehicle. That difference is the deal. Second, the break-even is $107B on the baseline — Prometheus must become larger than all but a handful of software companies on earth before we recover our capital. Fees alone, with no dilution, put break-even at $63B. Third, set years to liquidity at 20 and watch the IRR: Blue Origin has returned nothing to an outside investor in 26 years and SpaceX took 24 years to list. A long hold here is not the pessimistic case, it is the precedent.

Do not argue with the probabilities. Invert them. Hold the 42% bear weight fixed and solve for the conviction needed on the upside: reaching even a 2.0x expected net multiple requires roughly a 30% probability that Prometheus is worth $500B or more, with essentially the entire remainder on $250B and nothing below it. A 3.0x expectation is unreachable at any distribution over these outcomes without first shrinking the bad tail — which means fixing the structure, not believing harder in the company. Twenty-five companies on earth are worth $500B. The claim required is that this one has close to a one-in-three chance of joining them from a standing start, and someone has to say that sentence out loud in the room.

§22 · Recommendation

Pass, and stay warm. Prometheus may well become an important company; the team is the best in this book by a wide margin and the problem they have chosen is correctly identified. The pass is not a prediction about the company. It is that a pre-revenue business at a $41B mark, bought through an unaffiliated three-layer vehicle with no evidence of company consent, no information rights, no pro rata, no QSBS and no identified liquidity path, is not a venture position at any cheque size this fund could write.

Three things make this the most interesting file in the current VC series. The founding pair is genuinely exceptional — Vik Bajaj's career sits exactly at the junction of physical science and machine learning, and the technical bench came from OpenAI, DeepMind, Meta and xAI. The thesis identifies the right scarce input: proprietary physical-experiment data really would be a moat that a pure software company could not reproduce. And the institutional syndicate is not naive; JPMorgan, Goldman Sachs, BlackRock, DST Global and ARCH all wrote cheques at this mark after diligence we have not seen.

Four things hold it at 4.0. The entry price already contains the success: at the multiples this sector actually clears, $41B assumes $4.8B to $10.2B of revenue that does not exist, and at the richest multiple ever paid in engineering software it still assumes $2.6B. The data the thesis depends on does not exist and the plan to obtain it is a second, larger, unclosed financing. The technical precedent for autonomous physical design holds only where evaluation is cheap, fast and digital, and the company's own headline example — a jet engine — satisfies none of those conditions. And the instrument takes roughly half of whatever is left, with a binary risk on top that is not priced anywhere.

Would I take this at $41B directly on the cap table, with information rights and pro rata? As a small, deliberate, sub-1% portfolio position — possibly yes, after two answers: a reproducible benchmark, and a named arm's-length customer. Even then the expected net multiple is around 2.5x on a decade-plus hold with no liquidity mechanism, which is at or below what the late-stage venture asset class returns as a whole, for a single undiversified position. That corner of the table is available to BlackRock. It is not available to us.

Would I take it through the vehicle actually on offer? No, and not at any size. The probability-weighted net multiple is below 1.0x, roughly two thirds of the outcome distribution returns less than cost, and the base case — Prometheus becoming a $100B company, an extraordinary result from nothing — still loses money after dilution and fees. A deal whose base case is a loss is not a deal with a sizing problem. It is the wrong deal.

One thing deserves saying without hedging, because it is the finding this memo would want carried forward. The circulated memorandum is a good piece of company analysis and its arithmetic is correct to the cent; we reproduced every multiple in it. It reaches the wrong conclusion because it underwrites a company when the committee has to underwrite a trade. The instrument, the fee stack, the dilution path and the holding period are not details that refine the answer at the margin. Each of them independently reverses it. That gap — between analysing an asset and underwriting a position in it — is the transferable lesson here, and it will recur on every late-stage access offer that reaches this fund.

This memo was produced without company access, without a data room and without management contact, from public sources and a circulated third-party document. That is a material limitation and it is why the call is Stay Warm rather than a permanent close. It is also, in itself, informative: a $41B company that has disclosed no product, no customer and no benchmark cannot be underwritten to an investment standard by anyone outside its cap table, which is a reason for a fund of this size to decline rather than a reason to reach.
