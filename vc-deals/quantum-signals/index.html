§04 · What the company actually does

Quantum Signals trains a transformer model — the Microstructure Foundation Model, built on the published Temporal Fusion Transformer architecture — directly on Level II limit-order-book data: bid/ask depth and order-flow imbalance at tick resolution, for a slate of liquid futures and ETFs (equity indices, metals, with energy, rates, FX, and single equities on the roadmap). The model outputs a per-minute UP/STABLE/DOWN prediction to the closing bell, delivered live over a streaming API. Customers use it three ways: as a standalone signal traded on a fixed schedule, as a conviction filter layered on an existing strategy, or as an execution-timing input for VWAP/TWAP algorithms.

The commercial bet is distributional, not just technical: the company is not claiming to out-predict Citadel or Two Sigma. It is claiming that the roughly 1,000s of hedge funds, banks, CTAs, and execution desks that cannot build Level II microstructure AI in-house will pay a subscription for a packaged version of what the largest players already run privately.

§05 · Analogy anchor

A market-data infrastructure vendor's distribution model, applied to a prediction instead of a data feed

The analogy that fits best is a company like Databento — which sells normalized, low-latency tick data to funds that cannot build their own exchange-connectivity stack — except one layer downstream: Quantum Signals sells the prediction built on top of that data, not the data itself.

Where the analogy breaks, and this is the investment question. A data feed's value does not depend on how many other people buy it — every subscriber gets the same clean tick stream and that is fine. A predictive trading signal's value depends partly on secrecy: the more subscribers act on the same directional call at the same moment, the more the edge that call was capturing gets arbitraged away. The company's own blog post on this concern argues that usage diversity and market depth will blunt crowding, but offers no data — only reasoning. Whether "signals as a service" is a durable business model or a business model that decays as it succeeds is the central open question, and it is not answerable from the documents in this file.

§06 · Market, rebuilt from the bottom

The "everyone else" pool runs ahead of the primary source it partly cites

The deck frames the market as two tiers: roughly 20–30 Tier-1 funds with ~$700B combined AUM that build this in-house CO, versus "1,000s" of other funds, banks, and CTAs with "~$6T+" combined AUM that cannot CO, sourced in the deck's own footnote to LCH Investments, Form ADV filings, HFR's Q4 2025 report ($5.15T total industry AUM, per the footnote itself), and BarclayHedge's CTA universe (~$340B).

Independently, HFR's public commentary confirms global hedge fund industry capital "surged past" roughly $5T in 2025 EXT. If Tier-1 funds are already inside that $5T figure — which is how HFR's own reporting is structured — then the non-Tier-1 remainder is closer to $4.3T, not the "$6T+" the deck states for that same segment. The gap looks like it comes from blending in bank prop desks and other systematic capital outside HFR's hedge-fund count; that may be defensible, but it is not shown, and the deck's own footnote points to a total that is smaller than the number in the headline.

§10 · Competitive position and the capital gap

A genuinely unclaimed niche — which cuts both ways

A targeted search did not surface a direct competitor productizing Level II order-book prediction as a subscription API for non-Tier-1 funds. The closest adjacents are structurally different: Databento sells normalized tick data and connectivity, not predictions — a potential build-vs-buy substitute rather than a rival EXT. Kavout sells "Smart Signals" for equities on daily-to-multiday horizons, not intraday microstructure EXT. Numerai sources externally submitted models under a crowd-tournament structure, a different business model entirely EXT. The Tier-1 funds the company positions against (Citadel, Two Sigma, Renaissance, and similar) build this in-house and do not sell it — they are the reason the market exists, not a competitor for it today.

The steelman, and it is a real one. If no one else has packaged this, the company's "we are the only one bringing Tier-1-grade microstructure intelligence to everyone else" claim survives our check better than most of the claims in this file. The flip side: an unclaimed niche this specific is unclaimed either because it is hard (a real moat) or because the willingness to pay for it outside the top 20–30 funds has not actually been proven yet (a real risk) — and the traction data in §08 does not yet settle which.

§12 · Team

The technical credential is as strong as this house has seen in the current book: Iordanis Kerenidis holds a real PhD in computer science from UC Berkeley under Umesh Vazirani, a postdoc at MIT under Peter Shor, twenty years as a CNRS research director, an ERC Starting Grant, and 90+ independently findable publications, several with named industrial co-authors (JPMorgan, Roche) EXT. Yianni Gamvros brings a real, verifiable enterprise sales record — ten years at IBM, then Head of Business Development at QC Ware, where his résumé and independent press coverage corroborate growing a book of business from $0 to over $6M EXT. Both founders overlapped at QC Ware before co-founding Quantum Signals, which is a credible, well-matched pairing.

Where the framing runs ahead of the record. The deck's "took a QC startup from $0 to $6M ARR" implies Gamvros built and exited a company. He did not — he built a book of business as an employee of QC Ware, a company he did not found. The underlying fact checks out, so this is not itself a fabrication. It is nonetheless the same instinct — dress up an employee accomplishment as a founder accomplishment — that shows up elsewhere in this file as harder contradictions, and it is worth naming for what it is.

Advisors named in the deck — Chris Murray (Head of Trading, Jiko; ex-Goldman, ex-JPMorgan), Petr Merkuryev, CFA, and Christian Reuss, CFA (former CEO, SIX Swiss Exchange) — were not independently re-verified beyond the deck itself; Murray also appears on the cap table as a BSA/BSPCE holder, which is at least internally consistent. Team size is reported at roughly 5 people by a third-party scouting note UNV, thin against a roadmap spanning classical model, agents, and quantum hardware integration through 2029.

§13 · Go-to-market credibility

The top of the funnel is real and well-targeted: the conference circuit (BattleFin, Battle of the Quants, FutureAlpha, Neudata) is exactly where non-Tier-1 quant desks and CTAs are found, and the lead list includes credible, recognizable institutions. What the exports do not show is a repeatable path from "Lead" to "Paying" — of well over a hundred logged contacts across two CRM exports, only a handful have advanced past the earliest pipeline stages, and only two are confirmed paying.

Enterprise sales into banks and large funds typically carries a long, compliance-heavy cycle that is not obviously modeled in the company's own 24-month ramp, which assumes new-logo cadence accelerating well beyond anything shown in the pipeline exports to date.

This is the most testable near-term risk in the file: the ratio of trial-to-paid conversion is observable every month from an ordinary investor update and needs no special access.

§21 · Recommendation

Watch — Diligence. This is not a judgment that the company will fail, or that the founders are acting in bad faith. Quantum Signals pairs a rare, independently verified research credential with a real enterprise sales operator, sits in a genuinely underserved niche with no direct off-the-shelf competitor found, and has real — if very early — paying customers. That combination is worth staying close to.

The reason this is not further along the call spectrum is that five separate claims in the company's own materials contradict other documents the company itself provided, and three more cannot be tied to any source at all. The largest of these — a Delaware entity with 9x the French parent's revenue, undisclosed in response to a direct question about corporate structure — is serious enough on its own to require a written answer before any further step. The traction slide's gap against the company's own CRM is nearly as serious. Neither is proof of bad intent; both are proof that the numbers in front of a committee today cannot be relied on without independent reconciliation.

Would I put my own money in at today's information? No, and not because of price — there isn't one yet. The path forward is straightforward and entirely in the company's control: produce a consolidated financial statement, reconcile the traction and performance numbers against the company's own files, and clear up who actually held pre-seed equity. If those come back clean, this becomes a genuinely attractive underwrite on team and niche alone, and the composite likely moves toward 7 or higher. Ask for it before the round prices, not after.
